Tours & the owner's calendar

A renter asks to see a home — on a video call or in person. An owner keeps a calendar that says when they are free and for how long. What happens between those two sentences is most of this document.

Status: proposal Depends on: docs/application-process.html Audience: product, backend, both dashboards
Part zero

What exists today

Nothing — and one absence matters more than the rest.

The foundation that is missing

Nothing in Ampl knows what time zone anything is in. There is no timezone on a user, an owner or a property, and one astimezone(utc) call in the entire codebase. Every timestamp we store is an instant; not one of them is a wall clock.

That is fine for "created 3 days ago" and fatal for "2pm on the 13th". A calendar is the first feature where the difference is the whole feature — a renter in Mumbai reading a Toronto owner's 2pm, and a weekly rule that has to survive the Sunday the clocks move. Everything in Part 2 is built on getting this right first.

Part one

The shape, end to end

One picture of the whole thing before any of the detail.

One change from the obvious design

The middle box says propose other times, plural. The obvious version is a counter-offer of one time, which is simply the same round trip again with the roles swapped — and two people with busy weeks can bounce a single suggestion back and forth for days. Two or three options collapses it: the renter picks one and the tour is booked in that tap, with no third message.

Part two

The owner's calendar

Three settings and one rule. The settings are what the owner fills in; the rule is what turns them into bookable slots.

The three settings

SettingWhat it meansWhy it has to exist
Windows "Mon–Fri 10:00–18:00", or one-off "Sat 18 Oct 11:00–14:00" Recurring covers the normal week; one-offs cover the week that is not normal. Without both, an owner keeps editing a rule to mean an exception.
Slot length Per tour kind. Say 30 min virtual, 2 hours in person. They are different jobs. A video call is a call; an in-person viewing includes getting there, waiting for a lift, and answering questions in a hallway.
Buffers & limits Gap after a tour, minimum notice, how far ahead, max per day See scenarios 6, 7 and 12. Each one is a real way a calendar becomes unusable.
Whose calendar is it — the owner's or the property's?

The owner's. An owner with twelve properties still has one body and one afternoon. If availability were per property, the same 2pm would be bookable twelve times over and the owner would be double-booked by design.

So windows belong to the owner, and a booking on any property blocks that time across all of them. Per-property settings still make sense for the things that really are about the property — whether it can be toured in person at all, and whether somebody is living there.

The one rule that makes it work

A slot is bookable when it is inside a window, not overlapping anything already booked or held, and past the minimum notice. That is the whole calculation, and it has to run on the server — a client that computes its own list of free slots will be wrong the moment somebody else books one while the page is open.

Part three

A request's life

Six states. Two of them are the ones people forget.

Why requested holds the slot

This is the decision the whole feature turns on, and both obvious answers are wrong.

If a request does not hold the slot: two renters both ask for 2pm, the owner accepts both, and one of them turns up to a video call that is not happening. The system produced that.

If a request holds it forever: one person asks for every slot in October and the calendar is dead, for free, with no account needed beyond an email.

So it holds softly: the slot is reserved while the request is open, and the hold expires on its own — twenty-four hours, or when the slot's notice period runs out, whichever is sooner. A cap on open requests per renter does the rest.

Part four

Two rules not ours to choose

One comes from law, one from a promise Ampl already made.

1 · An occupied unit cannot be shown on short notice

Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act requires a landlord to give the person currently living there at least 24 hours' written notice before entering to show the unit, stating the day and a time between 8am and 8pm.

A calendar that offers "today at 4pm" on an occupied unit is inviting an owner to break that, and Ampl would be the tool that scheduled it. We do not even record whether somebody lives there — see Part 0.

So: the listing gains "is anybody living there now?". If yes, in-person slots enforce a minimum 24-hour notice regardless of the owner's own setting, only offer 8am–8pm, and the confirmation generates the notice text the owner can send. Virtual tours are unaffected — nobody is entering anything.

2 · An in-person tour needs the address, and we withhold it

docs/application-process.html, Part 4 is explicit: the exact address is released when an application is approved, and never before. An in-person tour is a person standing at a door two weeks before any approval exists.

There is no clever way around this, so it is a decision rather than a workaround: a confirmed in-person tour is a second unlock — for that renter, for that property, released at confirmation.

The narrower alternative is to release it a few hours before the tour and re-lock afterwards if the tour is cancelled. That is tidier on paper and worse in practice: somebody planning their Saturday needs to know on Tuesday whether the place is a twenty-minute walk or two buses. Confirming an in-person tour is already a deliberate act by the owner — which is exactly the gate the address rule was asking for.

Part five

The pressure test

Sixteen ways this breaks. Each one is a decision somebody will otherwise make by accident at two in the morning.

1. Whose 2pm? breaks everything
Owner in Toronto, renter in Mumbai. The renter picks "2pm" from a list. Nothing in Ampl currently knows which 2pm that is.
Do: the property carries a time zone, derived from its coordinates and stored on the listing. Every slot is computed and stored in UTC; every screen shows the viewer's own zone and names the property's — "2:00pm Toronto · 11:30pm your time". Never a bare "2pm".
2. The clocks change breaks twice a year
"Tuesdays, 2–6pm" is a wall-clock rule. Stored as a UTC offset it silently becomes 1–5pm on the first Tuesday of November.
Do: store recurring windows as local wall-clock plus a zone name, and resolve them to instants per date. The one hour that repeats and the one that does not exist are then the library's problem, not ours. Store bookings as instants — those are fixed points, not rules.
3. Two renters confirm the same slot in the same second
Both requests passed the "is it free" check before either was written. An application-level check cannot prevent this; it can only lose the race more slowly.
Do: a database exclusion constraint on the owner's booked ranges — Postgres will refuse the second overlapping row itself. The loser gets "somebody just took that one" and a refreshed list, which is the truth.
4. Two properties, one owner, same afternoon
A tour is booked on the Bloor flat at 2pm. The Dundas listing still shows 2pm as free, because its own calendar knows nothing about it.
Do: availability and bookings belong to the owner, not the property. One body, one afternoon. Per-property settings are only for things that are genuinely about the property.
5. Back-to-back in-person tours across town
2–4pm in Scarborough, 4–6pm in Etobicoke. Both are inside the owner's window and neither overlaps.
Do: a buffer after every tour, and a larger one when consecutive in-person tours are at different properties. v1 can use a flat travel buffer the owner sets; measuring the real distance is a later refinement and the flat number is already the difference between a usable day and an impossible one.
6. "Can you do 6:45 this evening?" judgement
Nothing stops a renter booking a slot fifteen minutes from now.
Do: a minimum notice the owner sets, defaulting to something humane — 12 hours for virtual, 24 for in-person — and forced to at least 24 hours on an occupied unit, for the legal reason in Part 4.
7. A slot booked for next August
A calendar with no far edge accumulates bookings nobody will honour, and a renter who books eight months out has learned nothing about a home they cannot move into.
Do: a rolling horizon — 60 days by default. Past that, "ask the owner" rather than a bookable slot.
8. Nobody answers the request
The renter waits, the slot stays held, and the day arrives with nothing having happened.
Do: the hold expires — 24 hours, or when the notice period runs out, whichever comes first. The request moves to expired, the slot is released, and both sides are told. The same shape as an application expiring, for the same reason.
9. Somebody cancels an hour before
Either side. The other has already rearranged their afternoon.
Do: cancellation is always allowed — forcing somebody to attend is not a thing software can do — but it is recorded, the slot returns to the calendar immediately, and the other side is notified through the channel they already use. A pattern of late cancellations is a fact about an account; what to do about it is a later decision, but the record has to start now or it cannot be made.
10. The owner deletes a window with a tour in it
"I'm not free Mondays any more" quietly cancels a confirmed tour, or worse, leaves it dangling outside any window.
Do: deleting a window never touches a confirmed booking. The owner is told plainly — "you have one tour on Monday 13th that stays booked" — and can cancel it themselves. A booking is a promise to a named person; a window is only a rule.
11. The owner changes the slot length after bookings exist
2 hours becomes 1. What happens to the 2–4pm already booked?
Do: it stays two hours. The length is copied onto the booking when it is made, not read from the setting at display time. A confirmed tour's end time must never move under the person who agreed to it.
12. Twelve tours in one day
A popular listing fills an owner's entire week with viewings.
Do: a max-per-day the owner sets. And when demand is that high, the honest answer is an open house — one slot, several attendees — which is scenario 16 and out of scope for v1.
13. A virtual tour with no link
2pm arrives, both people open the thread, and there is nothing to click.
Do: a virtual booking is not confirmable without a meeting link — the owner pastes one, or saves a default on their profile that is filled in automatically. Generating links ourselves means a calendar integration, which is a bigger piece; requiring one is a text field and closes the hole today.
14. A tour on a property that stops being listed
The listing is rented or unlisted while a tour sits confirmed on it.
Do: unlisting cancels open tour requests on that property with a distinct reason — "no longer available", which reads very differently to a renter than silence — and asks the owner what to do about confirmed ones rather than deciding for them.
15. A tour request from somebody who has not applied judgement
Perfectly reasonable — most people want to see a place before applying. But the thread and its card were built around an application.
Do: allow it. A tour request opens the same kind of thread on its own, and the thread's card shows the tour instead of the application. The card already renders live from the server, so this is a second shape for it rather than a new surface. If an application follows, both cards sit in the same thread — which is the true history of that conversation.
16. Three people want the same viewing
An open house is one slot with several attendees, and the model above is one booking per slot.
Do: out of scope for v1, and say so in the UI rather than approximating it. A slot with a capacity is a different booking model — the hold is per seat, cancellation frees one seat not the slot, and the address goes to several people at once. Approximating it with one booking and a note in the chat is how three strangers turn up expecting a private viewing.
Part six

Schema & API

What has to exist, and nothing beyond it.

New tables

TableHoldsNotes

Changes to what exists

  • listings: timezone (from the coordinates), is_occupied, allows_in_person.
  • conversations: already carries onboarding_id; the context serializer gains a tour alongside the application.
  • A DB exclusion constraint over each owner's confirmed ranges — scenario 3 is not solvable above the database.

Three departures from the list this section first carried, each made while building it and each worth the note:

  • /owner/availability is gone, folded into /owner/tour-settings as a windows array. The setup wizard writes the whole week in one go, so two endpoints meant two round trips that could half-succeed — a calendar with hours and no durations, or durations and no hours, either of which is a calendar that cannot compute a slot. Sending the week entire also makes the write idempotent: the server deactivates every existing window and lays down what it was given, so saving twice cannot produce two Mondays.
  • accept-proposal is pick, because that is what it does. The renter is not accepting a request for consideration; they are choosing one of the times the owner already said works, and the tour is confirmed in that call.
  • /owner/tours/competing is new. The comparison screen needs the trust score and the applicant detail for everyone on one slot, and a tour requester need not have applied — so there may be no application row to read a stored percentage from. It is computed from this renter's preferences against this listing, and is null when they have no profile yet. Never a fallback number: a constant is indistinguishable from a measurement to the owner reading it. The block deliberately omits date of birth, gender, nationality and country of origin — protected grounds in housing under the Ontario Human Rights Code, and a side-by-side comparison screen is exactly where handing them over would do the damage.

Two rules also changed shape once they met a real database:

  • max_per_day counts what is booked, not what is offered. The first version decremented a per-day allowance as it emitted slots, which meant the cap only ever bit on the second window of a day — an owner with one all-day window and four confirmed tours was still offered a fifth. It is now checked once per day against the confirmed tours on it, and a full day goes quiet rather than offering a slot the request would then have to refuse.
  • The buffer and the travel time pad the existing booking, not the grid. Stepping the offered times by length + buffer spaces out the slots on an empty day and does nothing whatever around a tour already in the calendar — so a confirmed 2–3pm left 3pm bookable, flush against it, and at another property across town. Each confirmed tour now blocks its own span plus the buffer either side, plus the travel time when both ends are in person at different addresses. Two video calls back to back get no travel time: nobody is going anywhere, and that is the owner's own business.

Jobs

  • Expire unanswered requests — every fifteen minutes, not hourly like the application sweep beside it. A request lives 24 hours and a tour lasts an hour, and both boundaries are ones somebody may be watching: an owner refreshing at 2:05pm for a 2pm viewing should not still see it listed as upcoming.
  • Complete past tours — mark completed once the end time passes. The same sweep, since it is the same question asked of a different column.
  • Reminders — to both sides, 24 hours and 1 hour before.
Part seven

Open decisions

Seven things this spec has taken a position on that are genuinely yours.

  1. Does a confirmed in-person tour release the address? This spec says yes, at confirmation. It is the biggest single decision here and it edits a rule we shipped a week ago.
  2. Tours without an application. Allowed here. Saying no is simpler to build and probably costs real applications — most people want to see a place first.
  3. Default durations. 30 minutes virtual, 60 in person are guesses. Owners should be able to change them; the defaults decide what most owners never change.
  4. Hold length. 24 hours. Too long and a popular listing freezes; too short and an owner with a job misses it.
  5. Who generates the meeting link. Owner pastes one in v1. A real calendar integration is a much larger piece and probably the right v2.
  6. Late cancellations. Recorded from day one. Whether they ever mean anything — a badge, a limit — is a separate decision that needs the data first.
  7. Open houses. Deferred. If a launch segment is high-demand student housing, that changes the booking model now rather than later.
The smallest version that is honest

If only one slice ships: a property time zone, owner windows with one slot length, server-computed slots, request → confirm → decline, the hold with an expiry, and the tour card in the thread that already exists. No counter-proposals, no travel buffers, no open houses, virtual only — which sidesteps both the address question and the RTA notice entirely, and is a working, truthful booking flow that can ship without either decision being made first.
